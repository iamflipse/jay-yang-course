Writing Words That Make People Buy

The Six P's of Copy Thinking

"The first step to getting good at copywriting is to follow what I call the six P's of copy thinking — the process I go through with all of my clients before I write any piece of content or copy: people, positioning, promise, proof, priority and process."

You can be handed templates ("use identity-based hooks..."), but "I would much rather teach you how to fish than just simply give you fish" — otherwise you repeat templates without understanding the why.

The six P's

1. People — who is the offer for?

"You can't answer 'why should they choose us' until you know who 'they' is."

Worked example — marathon training program:

  • Beginner who's never run one → promise: "you'll finish your first marathon."
  • Busy professional → "a training plan that fits your 60-hour work week."
  • Runner over 40 → "run the marathon while staying healthy."
  • Chasing Boston qualification → "get you across the line by a lot."
  • Coming back from injury → "help you run a marathon while staying healthy."

Get crystal clear on the avatar before anything else (see ch. 3 for the empathy map).

2. Positioning — why them over anyone else?

"Positioning is how your product is perceived in your consumer's mind. Strong positioning increases brand recognition, lubricates the sales process, and makes it easier for others to talk about you."

Seth Godin:

"Successful brands are built with your customers talking about you, not you talking about you."

Case: Claude Hopkins & Schlitz beer — Schlitz was dead last; every rival was making the same "pure beer" claim. Hopkins toured the brewery and found real differentiators (4,000-foot artesian wells, expensive filters, a 1,000-year-old mother yeast culture). He built the campaign around the facts of their process. They went from dead last to #1.

"You can use AI to write for you, but AI can't uncover the hidden advantages of your clients. Digging up those angles is on you."

3. Promise — what do they actually get?

Not features or deliverables — it's the outcome: who the customer becomes.

Steve Jobs didn't say "5GB of data." He said: "a thousand songs that fit in your pocket."

The "so what?" test: keep asking "so what?" until you reach the core outcome. Artisan rug example: 5x5 silk rug → "anchors your room" → "your room feels intentional, not random" → "visitors think you have great taste" → "you feel like someone with a home worth showing off."

"If you're not clear about the core outcome, your customers won't be clear either. And if you confuse, you lose."

4. Proof — why should I trust you?

"Trust is belief with evidence. Belief without evidence is called faith."

Three types of proof:

  1. Authority by association — "Elon says X," "Dr. So-and-so says do this."
  2. Your own results — "I scaled my business to $500K/yr. Here are the 5 lessons." / "I went from $8/hr barista to millionaire by 24."
  3. Client stories (the strongest) — "This is Sabrina. I helped her go from $50K/yr as a teacher to $50K/month as a high-ticket closer."

Proof is relative: match it to the avatar. Selling to a 50-year-old Asian woman? Show proof from similar women, not "a young white kid from Alabama."

The deaf-and-mute test: pretend you can only point. You can't point at "trusted by industry leaders" — but you can point at logos. You can't point at "proven results" — but you can point at a number: "2,600+ ads tested for a $106M-in-3-days launch."

5. Priority — why act now?

"There are only two reasons people act: toward something pleasurable, or away from something painful." He prefers pain:

"People will jog towards pleasure, but they will sprint away from pain."

Three levers:

  1. Scarcity — "only X spots left."
  2. Urgency — "this goes away in N days."
  3. Cost of inaction (his favorite) — what happens if you stay on your current path?

"The gap between where they are and where they want to be creates deprivation, and deprivation is the cousin of motivation. If you're drowning underwater, you don't casually swim up — you kick and claw your way to air."

6. Process — how does the offer work?

A good process does four things: explains why other solutions fail; creates a believable path; feels different; is easy to understand.

Finding yours: "Most people believe X. It's wrong because of Y. And the answer is actually Z."

  • "Everyone thinks you need to start a business to make money online — wrong, because you can get paid without being an influencer or building a whole business. It's called high-ticket sales."
  • "Everyone thinks more activity equals more progress — wrong, because you scale faster by finding and solving the limiting factor. We call this the value acceleration method."

Worked example: Alex Hermosi (course 1, ~16:24)

Fill in the six P's for your own offer (or a client's). The ad "almost writes itself" once the P's are in.
